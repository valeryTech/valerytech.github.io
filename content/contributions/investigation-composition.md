## The problem


A research interface can make its own representation the limit of the investigation. A matrix encourages one set of questions and combinations; replacing it with a cube or graph may preserve the same underlying constraint.

The question is which operations people need to explore a problem, and how the interface can support combinations that the designer did not prescribe in advance.

## The contribution


In the [edited discussion]({{< ref "ai/design-research/interactive-systems/03-composable-investigation-workspaces" >}}#initial-questions-and-proposals), Valery questions whether matrices, cubes, and projections would make possible investigations too rigid. His proposal is to provide small useful blocks while leaving people free to combine them, using Unix pipelines as an analogy.

This intervention changes the object of the design. The interface is considered through the work people can compose, rather than by selecting a more elaborate visual shape first. It connects a concern about human freedom to a question about computational abstractions.

## What changes in the design


The next design task becomes identifying useful operations, their inputs and outputs, and the relationships a combination must preserve. A matrix or graph can then serve a particular purpose within an investigation.

The model's subsequent reply develops typed operations, provenance, nested blocks, and constraints on valid combinations. Those are attributed proposals responding to the intervention. The [composition discussion]({{< ref "ai/design-research/interactive-systems/03-composable-investigation-workspaces" >}}#proposed-limits-on-composition) makes the resulting tension concrete: allow people to choose the investigation while making the obligations of each operation explicit.

The significance lies in the change of direction. A concern about how people think and explore leads to a different basis for choosing system components and representations.
