Leadership gives the team a problem and desired outcome; discovery tries to find a solution worth building; delivery creates the production solution; the team then sees whether the outcome actually happens.

## Action and learning


The "ready-fire-aim" criticism describes launching changes quickly and hoping useful outcomes follow. Acting first and defining success afterward makes the results hard to interpret and can turn general customers into unwilling test subjects. A fixed plan creates a different problem when the team refuses to revise it after implementation or real use reveals something new.

Discovery and delivery do not correspond to these two orders of action. Before a discovery test, the team should name the uncertainty, the decision it needs to make, the evidence that could change that decision, and the limits of the test. The test can then be small and fast. When it affects real users or systems, it also needs safeguards, observation, and a way to stop it.

Before a production release, the team needs enough evidence to accept responsibility for bounded behavior: who and what is supported, what happens when the system cannot provide that behavior, and how failures will be detected and handled. Delivery then observes real use and feeds what it learns into the next discovery and delivery decisions. The commitment can be narrow; learning does not end when delivery begins.

The recurring pattern is **frame the decision -> act within justified limits -> inspect the result -> revise the next commitment**. The distinction between discovery and delivery is the purpose of the work and the obligations it creates, not a fixed order of thinking and doing.
